Cloud Cost Optimization: Measure Before You Resize

January 30, 2026 3 min read

Updated September 4, 2026

A smaller cloud bill is only useful if the service still does its job. I approach cost work as an investigation: establish what a workload costs, understand why, change one thing, and measure both the bill and the service afterward.

Establish a comparable baseline

Choose a representative period and identify the owner of each major cost. Separate usage changes from rate changes, credits, and one-time charges. Where it makes sense, track cost per completed job, customer request, or another useful unit alongside total spend.

Record service health at the same time. Latency, error rate, queue depth, and throughput help establish whether a cheaper configuration is still acceptable. Keep the assumptions visible so another person can reproduce the comparison.

Investigate idle resources

Look for unattached storage, obsolete environments, unused load balancers, and resources left behind by previous deployments. Treat the list as candidates for investigation. A detached volume might hold the only recoverable copy of something important.

Confirm ownership and retention requirements before deleting anything. For an environment that is only needed during working hours, scheduling may be a better first change than resizing. Record a way to restore service if the assumption was wrong.

Resize against the bottleneck

Low average CPU alone is not enough evidence to choose a smaller instance. Check memory, burst behavior, storage throughput, network demand, and peak periods. A memory-bound workload can look idle in a CPU chart.

AWS Compute Optimizer recommendations can help identify candidates, but their performance-risk estimates still need review against the workload and the available metrics. Trial a change on a controlled slice and compare it with the baseline before expanding it.

Understand what Kubernetes estimates mean

My k8s-cost-radar project estimates request-based resource costs using configurable hourly rates. That makes a workload’s declared footprint easier to inspect. It does not measure actual utilization or reconcile the cloud invoice.

A large request or a high limit-to-request ratio is a reason to look at measurements, not proof of waste. Reducing a Pod’s request does not automatically reduce the node bill. Savings depend on whether capacity can actually be removed or reused.

Compare commitments after usage review

Once the likely steady-state demand is understood, compare commitment options with the resources that are expected to remain. Model lower-demand scenarios and migration plans as well as the expected case. A discounted rate is less useful when the commitment covers capacity the service no longer needs.

Keep rate savings separate from usage savings in the report. The two come from different decisions and have different risks.

Write down the result

A useful change record includes the original configuration, the new one, the measurement window, the observed cost difference, and the effect on service health. Label an annualized projection as a projection. Include any implementation or migration cost that affects the decision.

There is no fixed percentage I would promise before inspecting a workload. The deliverable is a measured improvement with enough context to explain why it worked and when to revisit it.
